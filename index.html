Research focus

Creativity in music education

My PhD asked how music teachers in China think about creativity, and what shapes their views. It draws on Csikszentmihalyi's systems model, among other theories. In that model, creativity happens between three parts: the Individual who makes something new, the Domain of existing knowledge, and the Field of people who judge it. The Field acts as a gatekeeper, deciding which new ideas get passed on. I used the model to look at how exams, the curriculum and teachers' own training shape their views, and the study produced an original seven-phase spectrum of music teaching and learning. China's curriculum standards name three areas of creative work: exploring sound, improvisation and composition. A related peer-reviewed article is forthcoming in Music Education Research.

A project of our own

Syn-Aesthetica — 2025–present

Dr Dong Ding and I started this in early 2025, soon after we both finished our PhDs. It has no funding and no commission. It comes from our own reading of where music, AI and physical interfaces are heading, and from curiosity.

The idea: analyse a piece of music (its sound features and emotional tone) and let a sculpture move to it. Dr Ding (jewellery design, Edinburgh College of Art) designs the sculpture's moving mechanisms. I write the code that analyses the music, train a small model to recognise emotion in it (using public datasets of music labelled by emotion), and design how we would test whether it helps people learn. It is written in Python and C++, and it is still a prototype.

We are sharing it early because we are both at the start of our careers and want to talk with others working on music, learning and physical interfaces. Two papers are in preparation: a scoping review of existing work on physical representations of music (Dr Ding leads; aimed at CoDesign) and a paper describing the software (I lead; aimed at the Journal of Open Source Software).

Education

PhD in Education, University of Glasgow · 01/2019–06/2024

Thesis: Music teachers' perceptions of creativity in the context of twenty-first century Chinese education (viva 27 September 2023). Supervisors: Prof. Oscar Odena, Prof. Kay Livingston.

Method: 17 semi-structured interviews and 24 lesson observations, plus critical-incident charting and a researcher diary, analysed thematically in NVivo. Result: an original seven-stage model of how creativity develops in music education. Key finding: conservatoire-trained instrumentalists received formal improvisation training, while generalist teachers engaged with it only informally.
